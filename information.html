SPOTTED LANTERNFLY

WHAT IS A SPOTTED LANTERNFLY AND WHY ARE THEY A PROBLEM?

The spotted lanternfly (Lycorma delicatula) is an invasive sap-feeding insect native to Asia. It was first detected in the United States in Pennsylvania in 2014, likely after being transported in contaminated shipments. It feeds on the sap of grapevines, hops, fruit trees, and hardwoods such as maple and oak, weakening plants and reducing crop quality and yield. The insect’s feeding can cause severe economic losses, including reported yield reductions of up to 90% in vineyards.

In addition to direct feeding damage, spotted lanternflies excrete honeydew, a sticky residue that promotes sooty mold growth. Sooty mold blocks sunlight, stresses plants, and can reduce tree vigor and productivity. The species also disrupts local ecosystems by removing food sources and reducing habitat quality for other insects. Because infestations can spread quickly, early detection and rapid response are essential.

WHEN AND WHERE TO FIND THEM

Adults are most active from spring through fall, while egg masses can persist year-round and survive winter on surfaces such as tree trunks, fence posts, outdoor furniture, vehicles, grills, and other hard objects. They are often found on tree-of-heaven, which is a preferred host, but they can also be seen on many other plants and outdoor structures.

Spotted lanternflies spread primarily by hitchhiking on outdoor items, firewood, vehicles, and equipment moved from infested areas. This makes inspection and prevention especially important in and around known infested zones. Early identification is critical because once populations become established, control becomes significantly more difficult.

HOW TO IDENTIFY THEM

Adults are approximately 1 inch long. Their forewings are brown with black markings, while their hindwings are bright red with black spots. Nymphs change appearance as they mature: early instars are black with white spots, while later instars become red with white markings.

Egg masses are about 1 inch long, gray, and resemble putty or dried mud. Each mass may contain 30 to 50 eggs and is commonly found on smooth surfaces from fall through spring. Common signs of infestation include honeydew on foliage, sooty mold, and clusters of insects feeding on host plants. Accurate identification is important for timely removal and reporting.

HOW TO REMOVE THEM SAFELY

Manual removal is often the most effective approach for individuals and egg masses. Egg masses should be scraped off from fall through spring using a plastic card or putty knife and then placed in a bag with rubbing alcohol or hand sanitizer to prevent hatching. Adults can be killed by stomping, swatting, or placing them in soapy water or alcohol.

Additional control options include placing circle traps around host tree trunks to capture nymphs, removing host plants such as tree-of-heaven where practical, and applying targeted sprays such as neem oil or vinegar-based solutions. Biological control measures, including parasitic wasps and fungi, are also being studied. Prevention is equally important: inspect vehicles, outdoor gear, grills, and furniture before travel, and report sightings outside known infested areas to local agricultural authorities.



TREE OF HEAVEN

WHAT IS A TREE OF HEAVEN?

Tree of heaven (Ailanthus altissima) is a fast-growing, invasive deciduous tree that is widespread in Virginia and is classified as a noxious weed. Native to China and introduced to the United States in the late 1700s as an ornamental street tree, it has since spread across all 43 states and is particularly common throughout Virginia, especially along roadsides, disturbed sites, urban areas, and in regions such as the Blue Ridge Mountains and the I-81 corridor. In Virginia alone, it is estimated to account for more than 67 million cubic feet of live volume, making it a significant ecological and management concern. Tree of heaven is highly competitive, releasing chemicals into the soil that suppress native vegetation, damaging infrastructure with aggressive roots, and serving as a preferred host for the spotted lanternfly, which threatens agricultural and forestry systems. Because of its invasive nature and potential harm, it is illegal to transport or sell without state or federal approval. Effective management typically involves herbicide treatment, mechanical removal, and ongoing monitoring to prevent regrowth and reduce its spread.

WHY IS IT A PROBLEM?

Tree of heaven aggressively outcompetes native plants, releases chemicals into the soil that suppress surrounding vegetation, and can damage infrastructure with its invasive root system. It also produces a strong, unpleasant odor when leaves or stems are crushed. Its prolific seed production and ability to resprout from roots and stumps make it difficult to eradicate once established.

HOW TO IDENTIFY A TREE OF HEAVEN

Tree of heaven is easy to recognize by its distinct foliage and growth habit. Its leaves are pinnately compound, typically ranging from 1 to 3 feet long, and may contain 9 to 41 leaflets. Each leaflet features glandular teeth at the base, helping to distinguish it from similar species. The twigs are stout and smooth to slightly fuzzy, and when broken, the pith emits a strong odor resembling rancid peanut butter. Young bark is smooth and green, while mature bark becomes brown or gray and develops a characteristic “cantaloupe” pattern. The tree produces abundant winged samaras, similar in appearance to maple seeds, and female trees can generate tens of thousands of seeds. It grows rapidly—sometimes up to 8 feet per year—and often forms dense multi-stem thickets. It also regenerates aggressively from stumps and roots, making effective control especially important.

WHEN AND WHERE IS IT LOCATED?

Tree of heaven can be found year-round in disturbed, sunny areas, but aerial surveys used for mapping are typically conducted in late winter, from December to January, after leaf drop when the tree’s seed-bearing structures are more visible. It is a common sight along highways, in parks, and in urban streets throughout Virginia. In summary, tree of heaven is a widespread and highly invasive noxious weed in the state. It can be identified by its compound leaves, winged seeds, and the distinct peanut-butter-like odor emitted by the twig pith when broken. Effective control requires a combination of repeated herbicide applications, mechanical removal, and ongoing monitoring. It is also illegal to transport or sell without the proper state or federal approval. For mapping information and local management guidance, Virginia Department of Forestry and VDACS resources are the most reliable sources.

WAYS TO REMOVE

Effective control of tree of heaven requires a sustained and targeted approach. For smaller trees, herbicide treatment is often applied after cutting, while larger trees may be treated with trunk incisions and herbicide while still standing. Mechanical removal is also used, but it must be followed by herbicide application to prevent regrowth from root suckers and remaining root systems. Ongoing monitoring is essential, with treated stumps and sites regularly inspected by agencies and volunteer groups to identify regrowth and new seedlings. Biological control research is still in progress, but it is not yet a standalone solution. Prevention is critical: tree of heaven should not be transported or sold without the required authorization. Most importantly, cutting alone is not effective, as repeated treatments are typically necessary to eliminate the root system and prevent reinfestation.



JAPANESE STILTGRASS

WHAT IS IT AND WHY IS IT A PROBLEM?

Japanese stiltgrass (Microstegium vimineum) is an aggressive invasive annual grass that spreads rapidly and forms dense mats of growth. These mats can smother native plants, reduce sunlight, compete for water and nutrients, and ultimately lower biodiversity in woodlands and other natural areas. It is considered one of the most damaging invasive plants in the eastern United States because it can transform diverse habitat into a monoculture over time.

The plant is especially problematic because it spreads efficiently through wind, water, and human activity, including movement of contaminated soil, equipment, and materials. Its seeds can remain viable in the soil for years, making long-term control difficult. In addition, Japanese stiltgrass can release chemicals into the soil that suppress the growth of competing plants, further helping it dominate disturbed or shaded sites.

WHEN AND WHERE TO FIND IT

Japanese stiltgrass is now widespread across much of the eastern United States and has been reported as far west as Texas. It was first documented in the U.S. in 1919 and is most common in moist, shaded, or disturbed habitats, including woodlands, forest edges, trails, roadways, floodplains, lawns, and other open areas with regular disturbance.

The plant germinates in early spring, grows through the summer, and dies back after the first frost. Although the stems and leaves disappear in winter, the dead material often remains as tan or orange thatch, which can make infestations easy to spot in the dormant season. Because it thrives in shaded, moist conditions, it often becomes established in areas where native plants are already stressed or less competitive.

HOW TO IDENTIFY IT

Japanese stiltgrass is a relatively small grass, generally growing 1 to 3 feet tall, although it can sometimes reach 6 feet under favorable conditions. The stems are wiry, often prostrate to erect, and may root at the nodes. Leaves are smooth, pale green, and somewhat broader than many grasses found in shade. Seedlings begin with a rounded first leaf and then develop narrow, lance-shaped leaves with a distinctive silvery midrib stripe.

The plant produces small greenish flower spikes from late summer into fall. In winter, the dead stems remain as tan or orange thatch, which helps distinguish it from many native grasses. It is often mistaken for other grasses, but its smooth leaves, wiry growth habit, and small flower heads generally set it apart. It is also useful to note that native grasses typically have rougher blades and larger flower structures than Japanese stiltgrass.

HOW TO REMOVE IT SAFELY

Effective control of Japanese stiltgrass usually requires an integrated approach. Hand-pulling or cutting plants before they produce seed is a practical option for small infestations, especially when done before late summer. Mowing can also help reduce seed production, but repeated treatment is often necessary because the seed bank can persist for years. For larger areas, pre-emergent herbicides applied in early spring and selective herbicides used on established plants may be appropriate, but labels and application instructions must be followed exactly.

Prevention is also an important part of management. Avoid moving contaminated soil, mulch, or equipment from infested areas, and consider planting dense groundcovers to suppress regrowth. Because Japanese stiltgrass can reappear from seed in the soil, long-term monitoring and repeated treatments are often required. It is also important to confirm identification before treating any patch, since herbicides and mechanical removal can affect desirable native species if applied incorrectly.



INVASIVE HONEYSUCKLES

WHAT IS IT AND WHY IS IT A PROBLEM?

Invasive honeysuckle refers to several non-native honeysuckle species, particularly Japanese honeysuckle, Amur honeysuckle, Morrow’s honeysuckle, and Tatarian honeysuckle. These plants were introduced to North America as ornamentals and later used for erosion control and wildlife cover. Without natural predators or environmental checks in their new range, they spread aggressively and outcompete native species.

These honeysuckles are a serious ecological threat because they form dense growth that shades out understory plants, limits sunlight, and can girdle or topple young trees. They also create monotypic stands that reduce biodiversity and alter habitat structure. Their berries are spread widely by birds, allowing populations to expand quickly. As a result, they disrupt forest ecosystems, reduce food resources for native insects, and degrade the overall health of natural communities.

WHEN AND WHERE TO FIND IT

Invasive honeysuckle is common in woodlands, riparian areas, disturbed sites, and along fence lines, roadsides, and gardens. It is widespread across much of the United States and is especially common in areas such as Virginia, where it has escaped cultivation and established in natural landscapes.

The plants are often most visible in late spring through fall, when they are actively growing and producing flowers or fruit. However, management is often most effective in late winter or early spring, when the shrubs and vines are easier to identify and before new growth begins. Because they spread easily from bird-dispersed seed, infestations can quickly expand into surrounding habitats.

HOW TO IDENTIFY IT

Invasive honeysuckle typically grows as a climbing vine or shrub with rapid, opportunistic growth. It often climbs trees, fences, and other plants, using its flexible stems. The leaves are opposite each other on the stem, usually heart-shaped to lance-shaped, and sometimes have serrated edges. The stems are hollow, green, and flexible, which helps distinguish them from some native species.

The flowers are small, tubular, and range from white to pink, often appearing in clusters. The fruits are shiny blue-black berries that are eaten by birds and spread to new locations. It flowers in spring and produces berries in late summer through fall. Correct identification is important before removal, especially because many native shrubs and vines can look similar at a glance.

HOW TO REMOVE IT SAFELY

The most effective removal often combines mechanical and chemical methods. For small to moderate infestations, cut the vines at the base and pull or dig out the root systems where possible. This is usually most effective in late winter or early spring when the plants are easier to see and before they leaf out. Repeated treatments are often necessary, because regrowth is common and seedling emergence can continue for several years.

If mechanical removal is not sufficient, selective herbicides may be applied in late winter or early spring, following label instructions carefully. It is best to avoid treating plants when they are flowering or fruiting, because that can increase the risk of seed dispersal. A long-term strategy should include monitoring for regrowth, re-treating as needed, and replacing removed plants with native species to restore habitat and reduce reinvasion. It is also wise to clean boots, tools, and clothing after working in infested areas to prevent accidental spread.
